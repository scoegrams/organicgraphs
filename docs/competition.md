Yes—it’s a good idea, but the broad idea is not unique. Several serious companies already build parts of it.

The opportunity is in packaging it for smaller, industry-specific organizations that cannot afford—or configure—enterprise platforms.

Glean explicitly says its Enterprise Graph models relationships across people, projects, teams, processes, and content. [Glean Enterprise Graph](https://www.glean.com/enterprise-context/enterprise-graph)

Palantir’s Ontology is even closer to the full vision: it models real-world objects, properties, links, actions, workflows, and security as an operational representation of a business. [Palantir Ontology overview](https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/ontology/overview)

Fibery can create a space from an AI prompt with suggested databases, views, and sample data. That makes it the closest competitor to your proposed wizard. [Fibery product overview](https://fibery.com/101-questions-about-fibery)

Notion and Guru already provide permission-aware answers with citations across connected business systems. [Notion Enterprise Search](https://www.notion.com/product/enterprise-search), [Guru Enterprise Search](https://www.getguru.com/solutions/ai-enterprise-search)

## Your potentially defensible difference

I did not find a prominent product positioned around this exact combination:

> Interview a business, generate its industry-specific operating model, visualize it as a graph, provide forms and workflows for maintaining it, and let the customer export the underlying knowledge as portable Markdown.

That combination includes:

- An AI business-modeling wizard
- Ready-made industry packs
- Automatic schema and relationship generation
- Org chart plus operational graph
- Forms employees can use without learning Obsidian
- AI-proposed updates requiring approval
- Graph-health checks
- Cited organizational questions
- Obsidian-compatible Markdown ownership
- A price and setup process suitable for smaller companies

That is a meaningful differentiation—but it is a **product-execution difference**, not a completely new technology category.

## The largest strategic danger

“Works for every industry” is a strong platform vision but a weak initial sales message.

A publisher does not want to buy a generic knowledge graph. It wants:

> “A publishing operations system that connects titles, authors, editors, freelancers, rights, manuscripts, schedules, and production files.”

A payment-services organization wants merchant onboarding, processors, residuals, commissions, and support cases. Several specialized products already offer those workflows, including [NMI Merchant Central](https://www.nmi.com/products/merchant-central/) and [Residualytics](https://residualytics.com/).

You will compete against both horizontal platforms and specialized industry software. A generic graph alone will not beat either.

## My candid score

- **Generic AI knowledge graph:** 5/10—crowded and difficult to explain.
- **Universal graph platform launched across every industry:** 6/10—technically interesting, but too broad for an initial product.
- **Industry-specific operating system generated through an AI wizard:** 8/10.
- **Starting with publishers and design consultants who have already expressed interest:** 9/10 as a validation opportunity.

Those existing conversations are your biggest advantage. Competitors having related products is actually positive: it validates that organizational context, connected data, citations, and AI workflows have real value.

## What I would build first

Do not initially build the enormous application described in the IDE prompt. Build a sellable pilot:

1. Staff and freelancers
2. Clients
3. Projects or titles
4. Deliverables and files
5. Meetings and decisions
6. Deadlines and ownership
7. Visual graph
8. Cited organizational chat
9. Spreadsheet and document import
10. Obsidian export

Then personally configure it for three real customers. Charge for implementation, even if modestly. Observe which structures and questions repeat.

Your proof should be measurable:

- Setup completed in under two hours
- A functioning graph generated from existing files
- Employees can find project context faster
- Missing owners and deadlines are detected
- Weekly reporting becomes automatic
- At least one customer pays to continue

Only after several pilots should the software attempt to generate any industry automatically. The pilots will reveal whether the real business is:

- A standalone SaaS product
- A service-assisted implementation platform
- An industry-specific operating system
- An AI layer over customers’ existing tools

The idea is good. The winning version is not “Obsidian graphs for businesses.” It is:

> **A business-in-a-box generator that converts an organization’s existing information into a living, governed operating model—without requiring the customer to become a database architect.**
